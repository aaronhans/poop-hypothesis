What is causing the poop in San Francisco?

We tested 14 ideas with a year of 311 reports. Street poop shows up where the camps are. It is packed into a few hundred blocks. It is not a dog-walking problem in home areas. We could not tell if it comes from people or their pets.

Camp reports are 311 reports of tents or camps on the street. Poop reports are 311 reports of human or animal waste.

Prototype to test the pets idea

A care-based plan: help the animals

This plan tests one idea the data could not test. The idea is that pets of unhoused people cause much of the poop. It goes with the wider plan in Step 8. That plan does not rest on this idea.

Healthy, well-fed dogs leave poop that is easier to pick up. Regular care gives owners the bags and the habit to do it.

Working guess: much of the poop on the hot blocks comes from the pets of unhoused people.

  • What backs it: poop reports cluster where camps are. Camp reports explain 85% of the gap between areas. Only 5% of poop reports are on blocks with no camp reports (Step 4).
  • What is still open: the data cannot tell pets from people (Step 1).
  • Why to be careful: everyone needs a toilet. Only some unhoused people have pets. Studies put it near one in ten. Counts range from 6% to 25% (research review, ASPCA). We have no count for San Francisco.

What we would do, at one regular stop on the hot blocks

  1. Hand out one steady dog food. A change of food can give dogs loose poop (Merck Animal Health, VCA). A steady food takes away one cause. Our own guess, not yet tested: loose poop is hard to bag, so it gets left.
  2. Tie poop bags to each food pack. Put a bin at the stop.
  3. Offer deworming and basic vet care. Gut worms can give dogs loose poop (Merck Veterinary Manual). We do not know how many of these dogs have them.
  4. Ask owners to feed at set times. Vets say set meal times help a dog go at set times (VCA, AKC).

Groups we could work with

These groups already do parts of this in San Francisco. Working with them would be faster than starting from scratch.

  • Vet SOS. Free vet care for the pets of unhoused people since 2001. It runs pop-up clinics. You need to book a visit. It says it sees more than 500 animals a year. It also brings pet food and supplies to the Mission.
  • Full Belly Bus. Its Food 4 Paws program gives out free dog and cat food. It has sites in the Haight, the Mission and other parts of the city.
  • SF SPCA. It works with Vet SOS on some urgent care (Vet SOS services). It has also given pet food through the Mission Food Hub.

These facts come from web pages. Some have no date. We have not called any of these groups. Check times and interest with each one.

Why food
It brings owners back each week. The bags, bin and vet care do most of the work.
What to expect
Fewer piles left behind, not fewer piles. Better food alone will not cut reports much.
How to measure
Count piles on these blocks before and after. Do the same on like blocks with no stop. Note dog or human. That also tests the working guess. 311 reports cannot show if a pile was picked up.

Step 1

First suspects, and one hard limit

Aaron began with three ideas. One: pets of unhoused people. Two: housed dog owners on walks near home. Three: a link to crowding and home cost. Claude added a fourth: people with no toilet to use.

The limit: 311 has just one category for this. Its code is human_waste_or_urine. It mixes dog poop, human poop and pee. No field tells them apart. We call them all poop reports.

Set aside to fit a one-hour limit

  • Photo check of poop type. The plan was to sort report photos into dog, human or pee. That would split pets from people. We dropped it for time. We also never checked how many reports have a clear photo.
  • Dog counts, such as licenses and dog parks.
  • Public toilet sites and hours. We added these later, in Step 7.
  • Change over time. This is one 12-month view.
  • Block-level math. Our main test uses census areas, not blocks.

So every chart below treats all poop as one thing.

Step 2

What earlier work had found

In June 2026 we looked at this for another project. That work left three warnings.

  • Poop and camp reports sit in the same places. So do litter and dumped beds. Some of the link is just busy streets.
  • Over time, poop and camp reports did not rise and fall as one. The link scores were near zero: 0.20 in the Tenderloin and −0.09 in the Mission.
  • 311 counts who reports, not what is there. The Mission filed 4.7 times as many reports as Bayview. Field checks found the streets were about the same.

So we added a check to each test: reports of full trash cans. They show how much an area reports in general.

Step 3

Where the reports are

    Three areas hold 55% of poop reports. They are the Tenderloin (22%), the Mission (19%) and South of Market (14%). In all, 4,619 blocks have a report. Just 233 of them hold half.

          Step 4

          The camp link

          Each dot is one census area. The city has 233 of them. Areas with more camp reports per person have more poop reports per person. That holds from the low end to the high end. Income does not split the dots.

          Is this just busy streets? In part. Look at blocks with a camp report each week or more. They hold 42% of poop reports. They hold 25% of trash can reports. So poop is tied to those blocks far more than trash is. But trash clusters there too.

          We then held four things steady: how much an area reports, how crowded it is, income and rent. Camp reports were still the top factor (0.58). Crowding came next (0.22).

          Step 5

          The dog-walker idea: income, home cost and crowding

          First, a map of where the pattern breaks. Say housed dog owners were a big source. Then many blocks would have repeat poop and no camp reports close by.

          There are 107 such blocks. They hold 840 reports, or 1.6% of the city's total. They are the best bets for a dog-owner source. They are spread through the Sunset, Western Addition, Bayview and Richmond. The flip side is even more rare. Just 23 blocks have steady camp reports and almost no poop.

          Next, income. We took the half of areas with the fewest camp reports. They hold 57% of the people and 11% of poop reports.

          Richer areas report more poop. But they report more full trash cans by the same amount. So richer areas seem to report more, not have more poop. Rent shows no pattern (12.2, 12.3, 10.7 from low to high). Crowding shows a small rise (9.8, 12.2, 13.2).

          Step 6

          A wrong turn on timing

          Reports peak from 7 to 10am. Claude took that to mean poop builds up at night and is found in the morning. Claude then said toilets should stay open at night. Aaron had a simpler idea: people report on the way to work. We tested it.

          On weekdays the peak is at 8am. On weekends it moves to 10am. Trash can and graffiti reports shift the same way. So the peak tracks when people are out. We dropped the night idea.

          Two things show the work-trip idea is not the whole story. Weekend reports drop only 15%, from 148 to 125 a day. And poop reports are still more of a morning thing than the others. On weekdays 40% come in from 7 to 10am. For trash cans it is 31%. For graffiti it is 23%.

          Step 7

          Toilets and homeless services

          Aaron asked us to map homeless services. People may walk from where they stay to get food. The poop may follow that route. We looked and stopped early. The city's open data has no good list of meal sites, shelters or drop-in centers. Its list of city sites shows just five run by the homelessness department. So this idea is still not tested.

          The search did turn up public toilets. They bear on the no-toilet idea. They are marked on the island maps in Step 3.

            Toilets are already where the poop is. It looks like the city put them there in response. So the two big islands mostly have toilets now. Night hours are thin. The small islands in Hayes Valley and North Beach have no Pit Stop at all. None of this shows that toilets cut reports. That takes a before-and-after test.

            Step 8

            Proposed solution

            Aim at the 233 blocks that hold half the reports.

            1. Close the toilet gaps. Do not add toilets everywhere. The two big islands have Pit Stops, but few list night hours. The Hayes Valley and North Beach islands have none.
            2. Clean those blocks on a set schedule. Do not wait for complaints.
            3. Give out pet poop bags and supplies through outreach teams. It is cheap. It covers the pet idea without waiting for proof. The care-based plan at the top of this page builds on this. It also names groups to work with.
            4. Measure it. Count before and after. Compare with like blocks that got no help.

            What to keep in mind

            • 311 counts reports, not poop. Camp reports come in the same way. So some of the link may be who reports.
            • These are links across places in one year. They do not prove cause.
            • The census caps income at $250,001 and rent at $3,501.
